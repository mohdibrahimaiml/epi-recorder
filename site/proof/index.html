Proof, not claims

Break it yourself.

Anyone can claim a file can't be changed. Two of these tests come with commands and output you can repeat; the third is the honest question they leave open. The second one is the interesting one: change a decision and rebuild the ZIP properly, so that every checksum agrees.

Test 1 · the easy one

Flip one byte

Take a sealed record and change a single byte in the middle of it, then verify. 

$ epi verify tampered.epi --policy strict
[FAIL] Structural validation failed: Bad CRC-32 for file
       'artifacts/seal_chain.json'
exit 1

Expected, and not very impressive on its own — a broken ZIP fails everything. The real question is the next one.

Test 2 · the one that matters

Edit the decision, then rebuild the ZIP properly

Change "decision":"approve_refund" to reject_refund, rewrite the archive with mimetype first and stored, and verify again. Every checksum in the archive now agrees with the new contents. 

$ epi verify repacked-edited.epi --policy strict

✘ SEAL FAIL — FILE ALTERED
Reason: Integrity compromised (file tampering detected);
        approval invalid: Approval does not match this artifact binding
Decision: reject_refund — Approved. Order ORD-9001 qualifies…
exit 1

The CRC passed. The structure was valid. It still failed — the approval no longer matches the record it was signed for, and the verifier says so.

Test 3 · the honest question

What if the key holder signs it again?

Someone who holds the signing key can edit a record and seal it again, and on its own the new file will verify. That is what the independent witness is for: at sealing, the record's fingerprint is reported to a witness the vendor does not control. A re-sealed file no longer matches what the witness recorded, and a record that disappears shows up as a gap in the numbering. The hosted witness is included in the paid EPI Cloud plans.

No install

The same check in a browser

Open the verifier, drop the sample file, and use the tamper lab to flip a byte in memory. Untouched, the seal is valid and the identity reads unknown until you pin the key. One byte changed, integrity fails. Nothing is uploaded.

What these tests do not prove

The limits, stated plainly

A passing seal proves the bytes have not changed since sealing and that the signature matches the key. It does not prove that every action was captured, that the decision was correct, or that the signer is who they claim until you pin their key out of band. It is evidence input, not a certificate. Whether it satisfies a regulator, auditor or court is for them to decide.
