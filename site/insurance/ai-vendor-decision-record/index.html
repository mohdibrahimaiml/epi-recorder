For carriers, and the AI vendors who serve them

The minimum AI decision record for insurance AI vendors.

One page per AI decision: what the model saw, what it recommended and why, which policy clause and version applied, who reviewed it, and what they did with it. It is the smallest set of facts a carrier needs to answer the NAIC's vendor questions — and it never asks you to hand over the model.

  • Version 6.0 closed for written comment on 30 October 2026.
  • The Big Data and Artificial Intelligence (H) Working Group meets at the Fall National Meeting on 16 November 2026, 12:00–1:00 p.m. CT, and plans to consider version 7.0 for adoption.

Why now

The NAIC asked insurers how they oversee AI they did not build.

The supplement is a set of optional exhibits a state examiner can send an insurer. Exhibit B is the AI governance program. In version 6.0 it asks, at checklist item 3n:

“Governs, monitors, tests and ensures transparency of AI Systems developed by vendors.”

The same question was item 3p in version 5.0. Version 6.0 renumbered the checklist, so cite the text and the version, not a number alone.

Three other parts of the draft push in the same direction. Exhibit B asks, for uses with a direct consumer impact, “whether there is a human in the loop … how the company assures itself the human review is appropriate, and how any exceptions or errors are found and resolved.” It asks “how the company ensures explainability and transparency into any AI models recommending or making decisions.” And checklist item 3l now asks whether the company “ensures human oversight is appropriate.”

Exhibit C goes one level down. For each high-risk model it asks how the model was developed — internal, third party, or a mix, with the vendor name — and, at item 6, whether the company purchased the model, whether it was developed specifically for the company, and “is the Company able to modify the AI model as necessary?” Items 10 and 10a ask for testing for drift, unfair discrimination and proxy effects.

The older requirement underneath

A claim file already has to permit reconstruction.

NAIC Model Regulation 902, the Unfair Property/Casualty Claims Settlement Practices Model Regulation, is not new and is not about AI. Section 4B requires that:

“Detailed documentation shall be contained in each claim file in order to permit reconstruction of the insurer's activities relative to each claim.”

Section 4C adds: “Each relevant document within the claim file shall be noted as to date received, date processed or date mailed.” That is the dates field, in 1997 language.

Two caveats. Model 902 covers property and casualty claims; if you write life or health, check the claims model your state has adopted before relying on §4B. And it is a model regulation, adopted state by state with local variation — not a federal rule.

The open question

The trade groups asked for a minimum. Nobody published one.

In comments on version 5.0, APCIA asked the NAIC for exactly this:

“Additional guidance regarding the minimum information insurers are expected to obtain from third-party vendors would help companies better understand how to satisfy governance, validation, transparency, and oversight expectations for externally developed systems.”

ACLI drew the other line, and it is a reasonable one:

“The Supplement should not imply that an insurer must produce source code, training data, model weights, proprietary validation materials, or other information that it has no legal or contractual right to obtain.”

Both can be satisfied at once. A carrier does not need the vendor's model to reconstruct one decision. It needs the facts of that decision, written down when it happened, in a form it can open without the vendor's help.

There is already a template for the contract term. In New York, Insurance Circular Letter No. 7 (11 July 2024), paragraph 37, says insurers should include terms in vendor contracts that:

“provide audit rights or entitle the insurer to receive audit reports by qualified auditing entities”

The same paragraph asks for terms that “require the third-party vendor to cooperate with the insurer regarding regulatory inquiries and investigations.” That is the clause that makes a record useful: the carrier can ask, and the vendor must answer.

Scope: the circular letter addresses AI in underwriting and pricing for New York-authorized insurers, so it is a template rather than a rule for claims in every state. Check your own state and your own contracts.

The record

Eight fields, one per decision.

Field What the record holds The question it answers
Inputs the AI saw The claim data, documents and policy text given to the model, and the tool results that came back Model 902 §4B — reconstruction of the insurer's activities
Recommendation or decision, and reasons The outcome in plain language, and the reasons recorded at the time rather than reconstructed Exhibit B — explainability and transparency into models making or recommending decisions
Policy provisions or rules relied on, and their version The clause cited, and the form revision in force on the date of loss Model 902 §4B; the claim-file rule. The version answers “which policy applied that day”
Model and version, and whether it changed The model requested, the version that actually answered, and the date Exhibit C item 1 (name and version) and item 6 (can the company modify it)
Human reviewer: agreed, changed or overrode Who reviewed it, when, and the action they took — signed, with their role Exhibit B item 3l and the human-in-the-loop question, including how errors are found and resolved
Dates and times of each step A timestamp on each step, taken from the record rather than added later Model 902 §4C — date received, processed or mailed
Created at decision time Written as the decision runs, not assembled in response to an exam request “Contemporaneous” evidence — the difference examiners can see
The carrier can open it without the vendor A portable file the carrier can read in a browser, with no account and no vendor login The access gap behind ACLI's comment — and why this needs no model internals
What it leaves out

No source code. No training data. No model weights.

The record is deliberately thin. It does not contain the vendor's model, its training data, its weights, or its proprietary validation material. None of that is needed to show what one decision saw, why it was reached, who changed it, and when.

That is the trade. A carrier gets a reconstructable record of the decision; the vendor keeps the model. If your contract has no right to the record, that is the clause to fix — not the model to expose.

Readiness

The checklist.

Ten questions for any AI decision, then four more for insurance: whether the reviewer agreed, changed or overrode the AI; which version of the policy was in force that day; whether the record was made at the time; and whether you would notice a change after handover.

Pick one real decision the AI touched. Answer from the record itself. Every no is a gap.

A worked example

Denied by the model. Paid by the adjuster.

The sample is an invented homeowners claim, CLM-2026-04417, with invented people and invented policy wording. It is generated by the recorder, not written as marketing copy. Read it in this order:

01 · Inputs

What the model saw

The claim, the policy clause E.2(c), the form revision in force, and the adjuster's report — each with a timestamp.

02 · The model

A denial, and why

The model recommends denial, citing the 14-day seepage exclusion, and records its reason and the clause it relied on.

03 · The person

An override, signed

The adjuster reads the same report, disagrees, and signs an override approving a partial payment — reason recorded.
